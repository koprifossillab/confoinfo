Proposed Sessions

Theme 1. Coastal, Shelf, and Deep Sea Marine Geology
1-1
Coastal to shelfal environments: processes and products
Conveners: Kyungsik Choi, Xin Shan
▼

Coastal to shelfal systems record the complex interaction of river discharge, tidal currents, waves dynamics, and episodic storm events. Interpreting these depositional successions remains challenging because depositional controls operate across multiple spatial and temporal scales, including variations in relative sea level, climate, tectonic subsidence, and sediment supply. The interplay of these processes can obscure the dominant mechanisms that preserve sedimentary signatures in the stratigraphic record.

Refining our understanding of coastal-to-shelfal systems is essential for advancing sedimentological and stratigraphic models. Moreover, it supports resource management, such as energy resources, aggregates, and Carbon Capture and Storage, while providing the scientific basis to navigate increasing anthropogenic and environmental pressures.

This session invites contributions exploring coastal and shelfal depositional systems across modern analogue and ancient successions. Studies based on field and outcrop observations, subsurface data, remote sensing, laboratory experiments, and numerical modelling are welcome across all climatic and tectonic settings. Submissions from early-career scientists are particularly encouraged.

1-2
Sediment dynamics and geomorphodynamics in tidal environments
Conveners: Sun Min Choi, Joohee Jo, Jun Young Seo
▼

Sediment dynamics and geomorphological evolution in tidal environments are inextricably linked through continuous feedback loops, requiring an integrated research approach to understand their co-evolution. These dynamics are temporally and spatially controlled by periodic tidal forcing, alongside seasonal fluvial and wave processes along longitudinal gradient. In the face of increasing human activities and extreme atmospheric events, these multiple impacts can significantly alter sediment dynamics –erosion, transport, and deposition– thereby disrupting long-term morphodynamic equilibrium.

Such shifts trigger fundamental morphodynamic feedback, where altered sediment transport patterns drive morphological changes, which in turn redefine the hydrodynamic and sedimentological behavior of the environment. It is no longer sufficient to study these processes in isolation; rather, understanding the coupled feedback between hydrodynamic forcing and subsequent bedform evolution is essential for grasping the trajectory of coastal system. Such integrated approaches will improve our ability to predict future coastal evolution and are fundamental to enhancing the resilience and adaptive capacity of coastal systems under future climate change.

This session welcomes contributions from field, laboratory, numerical, and theoretical studies that investigate sediment dynamics and geomorphology across micro- to macrotidal environments. We also encourage contributions that introduce new methods for observing sediment dynamics and geomorphology, as well as studies focused on predictive modeling that aim to bridge the gap between traditional observations and models.

1-3
Sedimentary environment evolution of Asian Continental Margin and compilation of related maps
Conveners: Xuefa Shi, Minoru Ikehara, Tae Soo Chang, Shuqing Qiao
▼

The Asian continental margin, located at the convergent and collisional boundaries of the Eurasian, Pacific, and Indo-Australian plates, is a globally unique composite continental margin. This region is characterized by the world's most representative systems of monsoons, rivers, marginal seas, ocean currents, and sea ice, creating diverse sedimentary environments such as deltas, continental shelves, submarine canyons, trenches, and basins. This special session explores characteristics, mechanisms and evolutionary models of these environments across various spatio-temporal scales. Central to this research is the development of diverse thematic maps, including sediment types, submarine topographic and geomorphologic maps, horizontal and vertical hydrological profiles, and shoreline evolution maps. Driven by science and technological advancements, research on the sedimentary evolution of the Asian continental margin now integrates remote sensing, GIS, numerical modeling, and AI to transform vast amounts of observational and analytical datasets into sophisticated visual maps. By synthesizing these advancements, this session aims to refine our theoretical understanding of margin evolution while providing critical scientific support for resource management, environmental protection, disaster mitigation, and sustainable development. We welcome a diverse range of presentations on these themes. We anticipate that this session will serve as a platform for productive dialogue regarding the evolution of the Asian continental margin.

1-4
Sedimentary Processes in the Asian Marginal Seas and Marine Environmental Evolution
Conveners: Chuanxiu Luo, Sui Wan, Yi Zhong, Shuhuan Du
▼

Asian Marginal seas are dynamic interfaces between continents and open oceans, serving as critical archives of source-to-sink sediment transport, ocean-atmosphere interactions, ocean circulation changes, sea-level fluctuations, and biological productivity. Their sedimentary records preserve high-resolution evidence of land-ocean interactions and past climate variability, offering unique insights into the mechanisms driving regional and global environmental change.

In the context of accelerating global warming and increasing human impacts, understanding how Asian marginal seas respond to past climate perturbations—such as glacial-interglacial cycles, abrupt climate events, and monsoon shifts—has become critically important for predicting future changes in coastal and marine environments.

This special session aims to synthesize cutting-edge research on sedimentary processes and paleoenvironmental evolution in Asian marginal seas, with a focus on reconstructing past climate variability, identifying sediment sources and transport mechanisms, and evaluating the implications for future climate scenarios. The collection of studies will highlight how Asian marginal seas record and modulate global climate signals and how sedimentary archives can be used to constrain model predictions of future change. Key themes addressed in this special session include but are not limited to: (1) Sediment provenance, transport, and accumulation in marginal seas; (2) Marginal seas record and amplify global climate signals; (3) Evolution of monsoon systems and related mechanisms.

1-5
Deep-sea sedimentary dynamic processes: Combining observations and sedimentary records
Conveners: Zhifei Liu, Jang Jun Bahk, Andrew Tien-Shun Lin
▼

Research on deep-sea sediments has evolved from the early perception that the deep sea is a “tranquil world” to the current understanding that there are complex and diverse dynamic processes. Gravity-driven processes (including turbidity currents, debris flows, and grain flows) along with bottom currents (including contour currents, tidal bottom currents, and internal waves) together shape the deep-sea sedimentary system. The key to understanding sedimentary dynamic processes lies in combining modern ocean observations with sedimentary records. Modern observational technologies (i.e., deep-sea mooring observation, benthic boundary layer monitoring) reveal details of deep-sea sedimentary dynamic processes, while sedimentary records (i.e., surface sediments and sedimentary cores) provide evidence of sedimentary evolution over multiple timescales. The combined study of these two aspects helps us understand the formation mechanisms from microscopic sedimentary structures to macroscopic geomorphological forms (such as submarine canyons, channel-levee systems, and sediment drifts).

This session focuses on the latest advances in modern observation and sedimentary record studies of various sedimentary dynamic processes from the continental shelf to deep-sea basins, especially in the Asian marginal seas. We encourage, but are not limited to, submissions in the following research topics: sediment source-to-sink transport processes, observation and simulation of deep-sea sediment dynamics, paleoenvironmental applications of sediment records, deep-sea sediment and global change responses, comparative studies of modern observations and paleo records, and application of new technologies and methods in deep-sea sediment research (such as granulometry). Through exchanges in this session, we expect to advance research in deep-sea sedimentology and provide new sedimentological perspectives for the development of Earth system science.

Theme 2. Source-to-Sink Study in Asian Continental Margins
2-1
Transport and burial of particulate organic matter in the ocean
Conveners: Baozhi Lin, Minkyoung Kim, Shigeyoshi Otosaka
▼

Transport and burial of particulate organic matter (POM) in the ocean transfer CO₂ from the atmosphere to the deep ocean, thereby regulating Earth’s climate on decadal to millennial time scales. The POM in the sea are not only derived from the marine organisms, but also from the terrestrial plants, soils, and bedrocks delivered predominantly via river discharge. The sink and burial of biospheric POM (marine organisms, terrestrial plant and soil) represent a net sink for atmospheric CO2. In contrast, the burial of petrogenic POM (terrestrial bedrock) has no effect in regulating the atmospheric CO2 concentration. In this session, we invite contributions that advance our understanding of the source to sink processes of POM in continental marginal seas and the open ocean. Topics of interest include, but are not limited to, the molecular and isotopic characterization fingerprinting of sinking and buried POM, quantification of sources, fluxes, and fate of POM across the water column and in marine sediments, interactions and mixing between marine- and terrestrial-derived POM pool, environmental and biogeochemical factors affecting the transport, degradation, and preservations of POM, geoengineering and enhancing carbon sequestration of POM, from the modern ocean to the geological past.

2-2
Application of Ocean Color Satellite Sensors to monitoring marine environment
Convener: Jongkuk Choi
▼

The session is focused on the introduction and application of GOCI, the world’s first ocean color satellite sensor operated on geostationary orbit. GOCI was launched in June, 2010 and had been in operation until March of 2021. The second GOCI, GOCI-II was launched on the 19th of February in 2020 along with Geostationary Environmental Monitoring Sensor (GEMS), a hyperspectral spectrometer designed for the observation of air pollutants and short-lived climate pollutants, onboard the Geo-Kompsat 2B satellite with 10 years of mission lifetime. GOCI has been widely used to monitor marine environments such as the total suspended matter, colored dissolved organic matter, chlorophyll-a concentration, harmful algal blooms, surface current movement, etc. In this session, introduction on GOCI/GOCI-II and its diverse applications and detailed studies on monitoring marine environment especially in the Korea coastal waters.

2-3
Source-to-Sink Transport and Fate Across Atmosphere-Land-River-Ocean-Sediment Systems
Convener: Hoi-Soo Jung
▼

This session focuses on source-to-sink (S2S) transport, transformation, and fate of materials across coupled atmosphere–land–river–coastal–ocean–sediment systems. We welcome contributions examining how diverse materials—including atmospheric dust, sediments, nutrients, organic matter, greenhouse gases, contaminants, and other natural or anthropogenic substances—are transported, transformed, exchanged, and ultimately stored or released across interconnected Earth surface environments. Topics include atmospheric deposition, riverine transport of sediments and pollutants, estuarine and coastal trapping and redistribution, air–water–sediment exchanges of CO₂ and CH₄, cross-shelf sediment dispersal, and long-term burial or remobilization in marine sinks. Field observations, geochemical and isotopic tracing, remote sensing, monitoring, and modeling studies are all encouraged.

Theme 3. Paleoceanography and Paleoclimatology
3-1
Marine sedimentary records supporting climate systems-models
Conveners: Shu Gao, James T. Liu
▼

In the research field of systems-theory based climate models, the existing GCM-type global climate models rely mainly on instrumental data, but the instrumental period is limited to a temporal scale of 10² years. Some additional data sets are available from historical written records, which cover a temporal scale of 10³ years with low spatial and temporal resolutions. As such, simulations on longer timescales face the problem of data scarcity. To date, the progress made in climate change research has been in the ice age-interglacial cycle and the effect of human carbon dioxide emissions; recently, short-term climate changes other than these two channels are being considered. An even more ambitious goal is to investigate and analyze the climate changes throughout Earth’s history, on various spatial-temporal scales and with different controlling mechanisms. For these efforts, the development of future climate system models requires data from sedimentary records. Oceans represent the main water body that receives sediment from the various sources; thus, the sedimentary records formed by modern and ancient oceans contain rich data information, including both climate change information and related process information, which await to be further explored. Hence, the purpose of this session is to discuss how marine sedimentary records can support future climate system models, with the following key themes: (1) the diversity, temporal and spatial distribution patterns, and resolution characteristics of climate information contained in marine sedimentary records; (2) climate change data derived from marine sedimentary records; (3) information on changes in external and internal drivers of the climate system contained in marine sedimentary records; (4) normal climate factor data obtained from the analysis of marine sedimentary records to reveal the “emergent” phenomena of climate change; (5) methodology to establish the process-product relationship of climate datasets; and (6) examples of deep-time climate simulations driven by marine sedimentary records.

3-2
Western Pacific Paleoceanography: Past and Future
Conveners: Li Lo, Kyung Eun Lee, Takuya Sagawa, Helen Bostock
▼

The Western Pacific Ocean, the largest and most dynamic ocean basin on Earth, plays a pivotal role in global heat redistribution, carbon cycling, and climate variability from tropical to polar regions. Its unique configuration—connecting major marginal seas, large continental landmasses, and deep-water formation regions—governs energy and material exchange across the Earth system. Over recent decades, extensive paleoceanographic research in the Western Pacific has generated transformative insights into monsoon evolution, ENSO dynamics, ocean circulation, tectonic–climate interactions, and marine ecosystem responses from the Neogene to the Quaternary.

This session aims to bring together new findings and emerging perspectives in Western Pacific paleoceanography. We welcome contributions presenting high-resolution proxy reconstructions, innovative analytical developments, multiproxy approaches, and improved chronological frameworks. Studies addressing paleo-ecology, biodiversity evolution, species adaptation, and extinction dynamics in response to climatic transitions are encouraged as well. We also invite research exploring land–ocean coupling, sedimentary processes, hydroclimate variability, and the role of tectonics in shaping oceanographic conditions.

Given the Pacific’s central importance in global energy transport and biogeochemical cycling, we strongly encourage contributions integrating data–model comparisons and paleo-numerical simulations from a Pacific-centered perspective. Abstracts proposing future drilling strategies, reanalysis of legacy cores, and interdisciplinary approaches linking physical, chemical, and biological oceanography are highly welcome.

By fostering cross-disciplinary dialogue, this session seeks to synthesize accumulated knowledge and stimulate new ideas on how Western Pacific paleo-records can inform our understanding of large-scale ocean reorganization and future climate change in a rapidly evolving Earth system.

3-3
New-Era Scientific Ocean Drilling: To improve collaboration in Asian Marine Geology
Conveners: Minoru Ikehara, Yoon-Mi Kim, Zhifei Liu, Ron Hackney
▼

Scientific Ocean Drilling, which continues to generate diverse scientific findings, entered a new era in 2025. In addition to the International Ocean Drilling Programme (IODP3), a collaboration between Japan, ECORD (European Consortium), and ANZIC (Australia and New Zealand Consortium), China will soon launch the Deep Ocean Drilling Program (DODP). The US has also established a Scientific Ocean Drilling Coordination Office (SODCO) in readiness for new, US-led expeditions. These ocean drilling programs are based on internationally focused marine geological and geophysical investigations.

We aim to use the opportunity at ICAMG10 to further advance international collaboration by sharing the outcomes of recently completed IODP3 projects and future drilling plans from all programs and consortia, thereby facilitating open information exchange. Presentations on new and existing ideas for scientific drilling in the Pacific and Indian Oceans are welcome from around the world, especially those that facilitate cooperation between existing and emerging programs.

Advances in Scientific Ocean Drilling rely not only on drilling expeditions but also on the utilization of six decades of legacy cores stored in global repositories. For example, projects are already underway and generating results through the Japanese community activity ReCoRD (Repository Core Re-Discovery Program), while the first IODP3 SPARC (Scientific Projects using Ocean Drilling Archives) expeditions are commencing in 2026, and the US National Science Foundation already accepts proposals for Legacy Asset Projects.

Outreach and training for young researchers is also essential. In addition to established ECORD summer schools in Europe and USSSP-supported initiatives in the US, regular international core schools are planned in Japan. We therefore encourage submissions that share and discuss recent information about outreach and training activities related to Scientific Ocean Drilling.

Theme 4. Marine Tectonics, Geophysics, and Geohazards
4-1
Interplay Between Fault Architecture and Coupled Processes in Subsurface Systems
Conveners: Soojung An, Jia-Jyun Dong, Hyeon-Seob Kim, and Byung-Dal So
▼

Fault architecture controls the evolution of subsurface systems through interactions among deformation, fluid flow, and energy transport. Variations in crustal strength and mechanical stratigraphy influence fault growth, segmentation, and connectivity, which define permeability structure and fluid pathways. Fault-controlled heterogeneity plays a key role in governing subsurface dynamics across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales.

Coupled processes involving geomechanics, multiphase fluid flow, and thermal transport are critical for subsurface energy applications. Carbon sequestration, hydrogen storage, and geothermal energy development require an improved understanding of fault-controlled permeability, caprock integrity, and fluid migration under evolving stress conditions. The interaction between fault architecture and coupled physical processes directly impacts storage efficiency, injectivity, and long-term stability.

This session invites contributions that explore the relationships between fault architecture and coupled processes in subsurface systems. Topics include field observations, laboratory experiments, and numerical modeling studies addressing fault development, segmentation, permeability evolution, fluid transport, and thermal effects. Contributions focusing on integrated approaches that link structural geology with fluid and thermal processes in energy-related systems are particularly encouraged.

4-2
Integrated Approaches to Coastal Geohazards: Observation, Monitoring, and Modelling
Conveners: Jun Yong Park, Jae-Il Kown, Jin-Yong Choi, Joohee Jo
▼

Coastal geohazards encompass a wide range of natural geological and oceanographic processes, including coastal erosion, landslides, flooding, sea-level rise, storm surges, tsunamis, and earthquakes. In recent decades, these hazards have intensified due to climate change and increasing coastal development, posing significant risks to human life, infrastructure, and ecosystems.

Effective management and mitigation of coastal geohazards require improved capabilities to monitor, understand, and predict the underlying mechanisms and driving forces under changing climatic conditions. This calls for comprehensive data acquisition through coordinated coastal and ocean observations, together with integrative research frameworks that adopt a holistic approach to the coupled atmosphere–geosphere–ocean system.

This session invites contributions on the observation, monitoring, and prediction of physical and geological processes associated with coastal hazards from interdisciplinary and multidisciplinary perspectives. We encourage studies presenting observational findings, including field measurements, long-term monitoring datasets, and case studies, which are essential for improving process understanding and supporting model development and validation. Purely observational contributions are also welcome. Contributions addressing innovative monitoring approaches, as well as advances in predictive modelling and artificial intelligence (AI) for coastal hazard assessment and forecasting, are particularly encouraged.

4-3
Seismic and sequence stratigraphy in marine sedimentary basins
Conveners: Kyongjin Kim, Donggeun Yoo
▼

Seismic and sequence stratigraphy provide fundamental tools for reconstructing the depositional architecture, basin-fill history, and stratigraphic evolution of marine sedimentary basins across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales. Stratigraphic records preserved on continental shelves, slopes, marginal seas, and in deep-marine basins offer key insights into the interplay among sediment supply, accommodation, sea-level fluctuations, tectonic deformation, oceanographic processes, and climate variability.

This session welcomes contributions that apply seismic reflection data, stratigraphic and sedimentological observations, core and well data, bathymetric and geomorphological analyses, biostratigraphic and chronostratigraphic constraints, and other integrated approaches to investigate marine basin fills and depositional history. We encourage studies addressing sequence stratigraphic frameworks, seismic stratigraphic interpretation, basin infill history, shelf-to-basin correlations, key stratigraphic surfaces and unconformities, facies architecture, and the links between stratigraphic development and tectonic, climatic, and oceanographic forcing.

Studies from modern to ancient systems are welcome, including regional case studies from Asian continental margins as well as broader comparative and methodological analyses. This session aims to provide a forum for discussing new data, concepts, and methods for deciphering the stratigraphic record in marginal seas.

Theme 5. Climate Changes in Polar Regions
5-1
Unraveling Arctic Fjord and Coastal Change: Paleoenvironmental Records, Modern Observations, and Future Perspectives
Conveners: Jung-Hyun Kim, Young Jin Joe, Dahae Kim
▼

Arctic fjords and adjacent coastal zones are highly sensitive environments where glaciers, ocean circulation, atmospheric forcing, terrestrial inputs, and increasing human activities interact. These systems provide key archives of past climate variability, yet they are undergoing rapid transformation driven by both natural variability and anthropogenic forcing. However, our understanding of their long-term evolution and future response to ongoing environmental and human pressures remains incomplete, highlighting the need for integrated approaches across disciplines and time scales. This session invites contributions on Arctic fjord and coastal systems, including Svalbard and the broader Arctic–North Atlantic and North Pacific regions. We welcome studies on sedimentary archives, glacier–fjord interactions, freshwater fluxes and hydrography, ocean circulation, sea-ice variability, and climate–ocean–cryosphere dynamics. Contributions addressing human impacts (e.g., shipping, resource development), as well as proxy development, numerical modeling, and data–model integration, are particularly encouraged. This session aims to foster interdisciplinary and international collaboration toward a deeper understanding of Arctic fjord and coastal change.

5-2
Antarctic Ocean–Ice–Climate Interactions: Paleoceanographic Perspectives
Conveners: Sunghan Kim, Min Kyung Lee
▼

This session focuses on paleoceanographic perspectives of Antarctic ocean–ice–climate interactions, highlighting the role of the Southern Ocean in past climate variability. We invite contributions that reconstruct shifts in ocean circulation, sea-ice extent, and Antarctic Ice Sheet (AIS) stability using marine sediment archives, geochemical proxies, and climate modeling approaches.

The session aims to bring together studies that address key processes shaping the Antarctic climate over geological timescales, including ocean circulation, cryosphere dynamics, and carbon cycling. Contributions spanning different time intervals—from glacial–interglacial cycles to longer-term climate transitions—are encouraged.

Topics include, but are not limited to:
- Southern Ocean circulation and water-mass changes
- Sea ice variability and its role in ocean-atmosphere exchange
- Antarctic ice-sheet evolution and ocean interactions
- Carbon cycle and deep ocean processes
- Marine sediment records and proxy development
- Past long-term climate transitions and their variability

5-3
Arctic Paleoceanography and Cryosphere Dynamics: High-frequency variability and global climate linkage during the Quaternary
Conveners: Kwangchul Jang, Xiaotong Xiao, Kwangkyu Park, Wenshen Xiao
▼

The Arctic Ocean and sub-Arctic regions have been key drivers of the Earth’s climate system through the growth and decay of ice sheets, freshwater discharge, and associated ocean and atmospheric circulation. This session explores the reconstruction of Arctic and subarctic paleoceanography and cryosphere dynamics, with particular emphasis on the glacial-interglacial variability. We seek to elucidate the mechanisms governing ice-shelf stability, sea-ice oscillations, and the Arctic’s role in modulating global ocean circulation. We invite contributions that utilize marine sediment archives to investigate glacial dynamics, sea-ice extent, ocean circulation, meltwater discharge events, carbon cycles, and sediment provenance etc. A primary objective of this session is to integrate multi-proxy (e.g., sedimentological, micropaleontological, geochemical) records with numerical modeling simulations. Such integration is essential for refining the timing and magnitude of Quaternary glacial-interglacial climate transitions and for identifying potential tipping points within the Arctic (and sub-Arctic) climate system. We particularly welcome studies that bridge the gap between regional circum-Arctic glacial history and broader Northern Hemisphere climate sensitivity. By bringing together sedimentary records and modeling approaches, this session aims to provide a comprehensive perspective on how the Arctic responds to and drives global climate change through the Quaternary.

5-4
Glacial Records and Margin Dynamics from Ice Sheets to Seafloor Evolution
Conveners: Su-Jeong Lim, Sookwan Kim, Byung-Dal So
▼

Polar and high-latitude margins preserve geological records of past glacial activity, reflecting the interactions among ice sheet dynamics, sea-level change, crustal response, and seafloor evolution. During glacial cycles, ice advance and retreat generated a wide range of geological signatures, including subglacial landforms, glaciogenic sediments, stratigraphic records, isostatic deformation, and fault reactivation, many of which continue to shape the modern seafloor.

This session invites contributions that examine glacial records and their implications for margin dynamics from the perspectives of marine geology, glaciology, geophysics, and paleoceanography. Topics include reconstruction of past ice extent and dynamics from seafloor records, glacial isostatic adjustment and crustal deformation, glaciogenic sediment transport and preservation, sea-level influence on margin stratigraphy, fault reactivation and seafloor instability related to glacial loading and unloading, gas hydrate dynamics across glacial–interglacial cycles, and paleoceanographic changes associated with ice-sheet fluctuations.

We welcome observational, experimental, and numerical modeling studies, especially interdisciplinary contributions that connect ice-sheet processes with marine geological records.

Theme 6. Submarine Resources and CCUS
6-1
Formation and Exploration of Marine Mineral Resources: Crusts, Nodules, and Hydrothermal Deposits
Conveners: YoungJae Kim, Jung-Woo Park, Sang-Mook Lee, Kiho Yang
▼

Marine mineral resources, such as ferromanganese (Fe–Mn) crusts, polymetallic nodules, and hydrothermal vent systems (including seafloor massive sulfide deposits), form through distinct yet interconnected geological, oceanographic, and geochemical processes across the seafloor. Understanding how these deposits grow, how they concentrate metals, and how their occurrence varies from local to regional scales is essential for interpreting seafloor mineral systems and improving exploration strategies.

This session invites contributions that advance (1) formation mechanisms and controls on metal enrichment and deposit architecture, and (2) exploration and characterization approaches that constrain deposit distribution, thickness, and grade. We broadly welcome observational studies (e.g., field mapping, sampling, and in situ measurements), experimental and analytical work (e.g., mineralogy, geochemistry, paleoceanographic proxies, and geochronology), and related modeling studies. We also encourage submissions on exploration workflows using marine geophysics and seafloor mapping (e.g., AUV/ROV surveys, acoustic and optical imaging, and data integration/analytics) that link deposit-forming processes to prospectivity.

6-2
Offshore Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS): Marine Geological Perspectives and Challenges
Conveners: Sookwan Kim and Byung-Dal So
▼

Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) is a critical technology for mitigating anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and achieving net-zero climate targets. Among various storage options, offshore sub-seabed geological storage provides a highly promising and scalable solution, utilizing the vast capacities of continental shelves and marginal sea basins. To unlock this immense potential, comprehensive exploration and a deep understanding of the sub-seabed environment are essential.

The successful and safe implementation of offshore CCS projects fundamentally depends on robust marine geosciences. The core expertise of marine geoscientists is indispensable across the entire project lifecycle, particularly in site evaluation, predictive numerical modeling, and long-term marine observation. Interpreting complex geological frameworks, simulating dynamic CO2 behavior, predicting potential seabed leakage, and deploying advanced monitoring systems are critical steps for ensuring permanent CO2 containment and mitigating geohazards.

This session aims to bring together marine geoscientists, researchers, and industry professionals to discuss the latest scientific advancements, technological innovations, and field experiences in offshore geological CO2 storage. We invite abstract submissions on various marine geological studies related to offshore CCS.

Topics of interest include:
- Site screening, selection, and comprehensive marine geological/geophysical characterization.
- Numerical modeling and simulation of CO₂ behavior and migration within geological formations.
- Predictive modeling and risk assessment of potential CO₂ leakage at the seabed.
- Advanced marine observation networks and Measurement, Monitoring, and Verification (MMV) technologies, including environmental baseline surveys.
- Stratigraphic, sedimentological, and geomechanical assessments of reservoir quality and caprock integrity.
- Geochemical interactions among injected CO₂, formation fluids, and marine sediments.
- Case studies, pilot projects, and interdisciplinary approaches to offshore CCS.

Through this session, we hope to share valuable insights and discuss the future direction of marine geological research for safe and effective offshore CCS deployment.

Theme 7. Geoheritage in Asian Waters
7-1
Geoheritage in Asian Waters: Scientific Value, Conservation, and Sustainable Use
Convener: Jongyun Jung
▼

This session invites contributions on the identification, assessment, conservation, interpretation, and sustainable use of marine and coastal geoheritage. Marine and coastal geoheritage is considered broadly here, encompassing coastal landforms, tidal flats, sedimentary and stratigraphic records, paleontological resources, and other geological features preserved within UNESCO World Heritage properties, National and Global Geoparks, marine parks, national monuments, and related areas. Special attention is welcome for Getbol, the Korean Tidal Flats, and other sites that illuminate earth system processes, sea-level changes, paleoenvironmental history, biodiversity, and human-environmental interactions.

Geoheritage shapes professional practice, public awareness, and personal connections to place. In marine and coastal settings, it contributes not only to ongoing research in marine geology, but also to conservation, place-based education, interpretation, and sustainable tourism. By making the link between geoheritage and cultural heritage tangible and teachable, place-based education and interpretation can improve earth system literacy and motivate diverse people to explore, value, care for, and protect significant coastal and marine landscapes.

We welcome interdisciplinary studies and case-based discussions on geoheritage inventory and assessment, criteria for scientific significance, conservation and monitoring, management with World Heritages or Geoparks, the role of geoheritage in tourism, and community engagement. This session aims to advance discussion on how marine geoheritage can be more effectively recognized, conserved, and activated as a scientific resource, a public asset, and a foundation for sustainable regional development.
